When I joined the Ministry of Women & Children Affairs as an Assistant Programmer in 2018, I was 23 and deeply idealistic. I wanted to build things that mattered.

I didn't expect to feel the same way building a live-stream auction feature in 2024. But I did.

## The government years

Working in government software is nothing like working in a startup. The pace is slower. The stakeholder landscape is more complex. A feature that would take two sprints in a commercial team can take six months when it requires sign-off across ministries.

But the stakes feel real in a different way. The National Helpline Centre (109) that I helped maintain actually answered calls from women in crisis. The resource portal I built was used by policymakers. There's a weight to that.

## The startup years

The commercial world moved at a completely different speed. At Studio Burning Bush, I went from onboarding to leading an enterprise team within a year. At Skribe.ai, I was shipping in the first week.

The problems are different — market fit, client expectations, revenue — but the core satisfaction is identical: something you built is being used by someone who needed it.

## Why I stayed

Software is one of the rare fields where you can go from a government ministry to an AI startup and the fundamental skill transfers completely. The context changes; the craft doesn't.

I've stayed because the problems keep getting harder and more interesting. Government systems taught me to think about scale, reliability, and stakeholder complexity. Startups taught me speed and ambiguity tolerance. AI is teaching me something new every week.

Seven years in, I'm still learning. That's the reason I stayed.
