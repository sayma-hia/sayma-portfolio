When people talk about supporting women in tech, they usually mean hiring pipelines, mentorship programmes, and coding bootcamps aimed at women already in or near urban, educated environments.

That's not a bad thing. But it misses most of the picture.

## What the SET for Women programme showed me

Building the e-training platform for SET for Women in 2020–2021 forced me to think about women in tech very differently. The women this platform was built for weren't job-hunting in Dhaka's startup ecosystem. They were in rural districts, learning vocational skills — tailoring, food processing, handicrafts — that could give them economic independence.

The technology wasn't the point. The access was.

## The two conversations happening in parallel

In tech circles in Dhaka, the conversation about women in tech is about representation in software companies. That's real and important.

But there's a parallel, much larger conversation about economic participation — and technology is a tool in that conversation, not the destination. The SET for Women platform didn't need to inspire anyone to become a programmer. It needed to help a woman in Sylhet learn a skill she could earn from.

## What I think "support" actually means

Supporting women in tech means different things in different contexts:

- In startups: hiring, pay equity, leadership opportunities.
- In education: access to CS programmes, encouragement to persist.
- In economic development: using technology to extend reach, not to require technical literacy.

The most impactful thing I've built wasn't a clever React architecture. It was a platform simple enough that a first-time internet user in a rural area could complete a course.

## Where we need to go

We need more women building tech in Bangladesh. And we also need the tech being built to be designed with rural, low-connectivity, first-time users in mind.

Those two things aren't in conflict. They're complementary.
