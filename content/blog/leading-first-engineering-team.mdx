The day I became a team lead, I thought my job was to be the best engineer on the team. I was wrong.

## What I got wrong at the start

I was still writing most of the code. I was reviewing everything before it went out. I was the bottleneck, and I didn't realise it for two months.

The first signal was a developer on my team asking — very politely — if he could push a small fix without my review. It was a fix I'd reviewed three times before. My instinct was no. My better judgment, after a pause, was: yes.

That was the turning point.

## What actually works

### Create space, don't fill it

My job shifted from doing to enabling. That meant reviewing architecture decisions rather than writing the architecture. Unblocking people rather than solving problems for them.

### Retrospectives are not optional

We ran retrospectives every sprint, even when it was uncomfortable. Especially when it was uncomfortable. The feedback loops from retros shaped how we worked more than any process document I wrote.

### Hiring is part of the job

At Studio Burning Bush, I was involved in hiring ~5 developers and 2 SQA engineers. I learned that the interview process is a preview of how you'll work with someone. A candidate who asks sharp questions in an interview usually brings that same sharpness to code review.

### Protect the team from noise

Senior stakeholders and client requests will arrive in a continuous stream. Your job is to absorb the noise and give your team clarity. If your team is context-switching constantly, something has gone wrong in how you're managing upward.

## The playbook I built

It's not a document. It's a set of habits: weekly 1:1s, clear sprint goals, retros that actually change things, and a bias toward trusting people over controlling them.

Nobody handed me this. I found it by making mistakes and paying attention.
