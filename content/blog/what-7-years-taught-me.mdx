Nobody tells you when you're learning to code that the hardest part of the job isn't the code. It's everything around the code.

## The first few years: heads down

When I started out, I measured my value entirely by what I could build. How fast I could ship a feature. How clean my code was. Whether my tests passed.

That's not wrong — those things matter. But they're not the whole picture.

## Communication is the job

By year three, I'd realised something uncomfortable: the engineers who got things done weren't always the most technically brilliant. They were the ones who could explain what they were building, flag blockers early, and make stakeholders feel heard.

I've sat in meetings where I watched a technically superior solution get rejected because it was poorly communicated. And I've watched mediocre solutions get approved because the engineer presenting them built trust.

## Leading taught me to listen

When I started leading a team at Studio Burning Bush, I thought my job was to have the answers. It took about a month of failing to realise that my job was actually to create the conditions where my team could find the answers.

The best thing I did as a lead was shut up more often.

## What I'd tell year-one me

- Ask more questions than you think you should.
- Write things down — your memory is worse than you think.
- Learn the business context, not just the technical spec.
- Your code is not your identity.
- Ship something imperfect. You can iterate. Silence is the worst outcome.

Seven years in, I'm still learning all of these. That's the point.
